The [Iris settings](/instructor/communication-support/iris) decide _how_ Iris answers — whether it is available and how
much it gives away. This page covers the other half: _what_ it answers from, and _which_ part of your course a given
conversation is about.

Both matter to you as an instructor, because a tutor that has not read your lecture cannot refer to it, and a student
who is asking about the wrong exercise gets an answer that looks confidently wrong.

<Callout variant={CalloutVariant.info}>

Everything here applies only once Iris is enabled for the course. If the **Iris** entry is missing from your course
management, an administrator has not configured an Iris service for the instance.

</Callout>

## The Course Knowledge Base

Iris does not read your course the way a student does. Course material has to be processed before it can be drawn on,
and until it has been, Iris answers from the exercise at hand and the general knowledge of its model — which is to say,
it will not cite your lecture even when your lecture is exactly what the student needs.

Two kinds of material are processed.

**Lecture content** is processed as you publish it. Attachments, slides, and transcriptions of lecture videos are picked
up automatically, so in the ordinary case there is nothing for you to do beyond uploading the material. While a unit is
being processed, the lecture unit management table shows it as **Indexing**; a unit that fails shows
**Content indexing failed after multiple attempts**, and its content is not available to Iris until it is reprocessed.

**FAQs** are the part you trigger yourself. On the **FAQ** page of the course, with Iris enabled, instructors get a
**Send FAQs to Iris** button that submits the course's FAQs for processing. Artemis confirms with _All Faq processing in
Iris started successfully_.

<Callout variant={CalloutVariant.warning}>

Sending FAQs is not automatic on edit. If you change the wording of an answer, or add an FAQ mid-semester, Iris keeps
using what it processed last until you send them again. Re-send after a round of FAQ edits.

</Callout>

Processing is asynchronous, so the confirmation means _started_, not _finished_. If Iris does not yet reflect material
you have just published, that is usually the answer, and waiting is the right response.

### Why This Is Worth Your Attention

An FAQ that Iris knows about is answered consistently, at any hour, without a student waiting for the communication
channel. An FAQ it does not know about is answered from general knowledge — plausibly, and possibly contrary to what you
wrote. Keeping the knowledge base current is the cheapest way to keep Iris aligned with your course.

## Chat Topics

A conversation with Iris is always _about_ something, and that topic decides what Iris pulls in before it answers.

When a student opens Iris inside an exercise, the topic is set for them: the exercise, its problem statement, and their
own latest submission are the context. When they open Iris from the course, the conversation starts course-wide.

Students can also set the topic themselves. The chat has a **Select lecture or exercise** control that lists the
lectures and exercises of the course, grouped under **Course**, **Lectures** and **Exercises** and searchable by name.
The chosen topic then appears as a chip above the input, which they can remove to return to a course-wide chat. Changing
the topic mid-conversation is allowed — Artemis marks the switch in the transcript rather than starting a new chat.

<Callout variant={CalloutVariant.info}>

The topic list contains what the student can see. An unreleased exercise or an unpublished lecture is not offered, so
a student cannot use Iris to reach material you have not released yet.

</Callout>

For you, the practical consequence is that a student's complaint about a poor Iris answer is often a topic problem
rather than a tutoring problem. "Iris did not know about the assignment" is frequently a course-wide chat that was never
pointed at the exercise. Asking which topic was set is a good first question.

## Following What Iris Did

While Iris works, it reports what it is doing: **Reading lecture**, **Reading FAQs**, **Reading problem statement**,
**Browsing your code**, **Checking build logs**, **Checking your progress**. When the answer arrives, those steps
collapse into a **Thought process** summary that the student — and you, in your own account — can expand.

Answers drawn from lecture material carry a citation naming the lecture and the unit it came from.

This is the most direct way to diagnose a disappointing answer. If a student shows you a reply that ignored your lecture,
the activity trail says whether Iris read the lecture at all. No **Reading lecture** step and no citation usually points
back to the knowledge base — the material was never processed — rather than to the support level or your guidelines.

## Checking It Yourself

Iris in your own account uses the same settings and the same knowledge base your students get, so the quickest
end-to-end check of a course's setup is to ask it something yourself:

1. Open Iris from the course, leaving the topic course-wide, and ask a question your FAQs answer. A reply that matches
   your wording means the FAQs were processed.
2. Set the topic to a lecture and ask what it covers. A citation naming that lecture means its content was processed.
3. Set the topic to an exercise and ask for a hint. This shows the [support level](/instructor/communication-support/iris)
   you chose, in the form students will meet it.

Doing this once after setting up a course, and again after a round of FAQ edits, catches nearly everything that makes
Iris look unhelpful to students.

<Callout variant={CalloutVariant.warning}>

Students choose for themselves whether to use cloud AI at all, and that choice is not yours to make for them. A course
cannot assume every student has Iris available, so anything essential belongs in the course material rather than only
in what Iris can be asked. See [Iris](/student/learning-content/iris) for the student's side.

</Callout>
