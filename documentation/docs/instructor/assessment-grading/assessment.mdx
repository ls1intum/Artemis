Artemis assesses an exercise in one of three ways, chosen per exercise when it is created.

- **Automatic** — Artemis marks the work itself, from test cases for a programming exercise or from the correct
  answers of a quiz.
- **Manual** — a tutor or instructor reads every submission and marks it.
- **Semi-automatic** — the automatic result is the starting point, and a reviewer corrects and completes it.

Manual assessment is available for programming, text, modeling and file upload exercises. A quiz is marked
automatically and cannot be marked by hand.

## How a Manual Assessment Runs

A reviewer does not pick a submission; they ask for the next one. Opening an assessment **locks** that submission, so
two reviewers never mark the same work, and the lock is what creates the result the marks are written to. The reviewer
then applies feedback and points and submits the assessment, or cancels it — which deletes the draft result and
returns the submission to the queue.

<Callout variant={CalloutVariant.info}>

  A reviewer may hold at most ten unfinished assessments in a course at once. The eleventh request is refused until
  one is submitted or canceled, which stops a queue being reserved and left.

</Callout>

An assessment lock does not time out. A submission stays with the reviewer who opened it until they submit or
cancel, so a reviewer who leaves work unfinished has to release it — or an instructor has to, from the assessment
dashboard. A complaint is the exception: a reviewer holds it for 24 hours, and the panel names the hour it comes
free.

Nothing reaches the student until the exercise's assessment due date has passed. Until then marks can be revised
freely; afterwards the results are published together, and students can
[rate the feedback](/student/assessment-grades/assessment#rating-feedback-quality) they received.

## Assessment Dashboard

**Assessment** in the course management shows how far the marking has got across every manually assessed exercise in
the course. Each figure — assessments, complaints, feedback requests, open locks and student ratings — is given twice,
as the course total and as your own share of it, so a reviewer sees both the state of the course and their own
contribution to it.

<Image src={assessmentDashboard} alt="Assessment dashboard with the per-exercise progress of the course" size={ImageSize.large} caption="Assessment progress across the course" />

**Exercise Dashboard** on any row opens that exercise on its own. This is where a reviewer works: it holds the
training material, the queue, and **Start new assessment**, which locks the next submission and opens it.

<Image src={exerciseDashboard} alt="Exercise assessment dashboard with the queue and the training material" size={ImageSize.large} caption="A single exercise, as its reviewers see it" />

## Structured Assessment Criteria

A criterion is a piece of feedback with a point value attached, defined once on the exercise. In the assessment editor
they sit beside the submission under **Structured Assessment Criteria**, grouped by what they judge, and a reviewer
applies one by dragging it onto the passage or the element it concerns rather than typing the comment again.

<Image src={gradingCriteria} alt="Assessment editor with the grading criteria beside the submission" size={ImageSize.large} caption="Applying a grading criterion" />

The point of them is consistency: two reviewers deduct the same marks for the same mistake and describe it in the same
words, which is also what makes a complaint answerable. Students see the criterion that was applied, so the mark
carries its reason with it.

## Training Reviewers

Before a reviewer may mark real work, Artemis can make them work through example submissions the instructor has
prepared. Reviewers see the training material on the exercise dashboard and cannot reach the queue until they have
finished it.

An example submission is created on the exercise, and **Assessment Training** on it chooses between two modes.

- **Read and Confirm** — the reviewer reads the submission together with the instructor's assessment and confirms
  they have understood it.
- **Assess Correctly** — the reviewer marks the submission themselves, and Artemis compares their marks with the
  instructor's. If they do not match, the differences are explained and the reviewer tries again.

<Image src={exampleSubmission} alt="Example submission with its assessment mode" size={ImageSize.large} caption="An example submission prepared for training" />

An existing student submission can be imported as an example, which is both more realistic than an invented one and
less work.

Whether or not there are example submissions, a reviewer starts at the exercise's own instructions: the problem
statement, the example solution and the assessment instructions, one after another, ending in **I have read and
understood the instructions**. Until that is clicked the queue is not shown at all, so the instructions cannot be
skipped past.

<Image src={trainingInstructions} alt="Assessment instructions a reviewer confirms before marking" size={ImageSize.large} caption="The instructions a reviewer confirms" />

## Double-Blind Assessment

Manual assessment is double-blind. A reviewer does not see whose work they are marking, and a student does not see who
marked theirs. Neither side can act on the other's identity, which is the point.

## Complaints

Where the course allows it, a student who thinks a mark is wrong can file a **complaint** on it. See
[Course Configuration](/instructor/course-management/course-configuration#complaints) for the settings that switch
complaints on and set their limits.

A student may have only a limited number of complaints that are **open or were rejected** at any one time — three by
default. An accepted complaint stops counting against that limit, so a student whose complaints keep being upheld is
never blocked, while one who files unfounded complaints runs out.

<Callout variant={CalloutVariant.info}>

  A complaint needs a manually marked result to dispute. Automatic results and Athena's preliminary AI feedback cannot
  be complained about, and in an exam a complaint is only possible during the student review period.

</Callout>

A complaint arrives on the exercise dashboard. The reviewer who takes it reads the student's justification beside the
original assessment and decides.

<Image src={complaintReview} alt="Complaint review with the student justification and the accept and reject actions" size={ImageSize.large} caption="Answering a complaint" />

- **Accept complaint** writes the response and updates the assessment. Adjusting the feedback blocks first is what
  changes the marks, in either direction.
- **Reject complaint** writes the response and leaves the assessment as it was. Say why: this is the answer the
  student reads.

Either way the response is written in **Complaint's response**, which takes up to 2000 characters by default. If
neither answer is right — the case needs someone else — **Remove lock** returns the complaint to the queue
untouched.

<Callout variant={CalloutVariant.warning}>

  Accepting a complaint can lower a mark as well as raise it, because it reopens the whole assessment rather than only
  the disputed part.

</Callout>

## More Feedback Requests

A student who wants their marks explained rather than changed can send a **more feedback request** instead. It costs
nothing against the complaint limit, and the reviewer answers it with a single **Provide more feedback** response —
there is no accept or reject, because nothing is in dispute.

Artemis stores at most one of the two per result, so a student who asks for more feedback has spent the slot and can
no longer complain about that result. That trade is worth making clear to students; the
[student page](/student/assessment-grades/assessment#more-feedback-requests) states it.

## Grading Leaderboard

The leaderboard counts what each reviewer has done: the points they have marked, the number of assessments, how many
complaints about their marking were accepted, how many requests are still open, how many complaints and requests they
have answered, the average mark they give, and the average rating students gave their feedback.

<Image src={leaderboard} alt="Grading leaderboard with the per-reviewer counts" size={ImageSize.large} caption="The grading leaderboard" />

Every reviewer sees it, which is the intent: the numbers are a shared standard rather than a private report. A
reviewer whose figures sit far from the rest — many accepted complaints, or an average mark well away from everyone
else's — is worth a conversation before the course ends rather than after.

## Automatic Assessment

A quiz is marked automatically as soon as it is over, and this is the only mode it has. A programming exercise is
marked by its test cases, either while the exercise is running or once it has closed, and can be marked semi-
automatically by having a reviewer correct the result afterwards. Complaints can be enabled for automatically graded
programming exercises, so a student can dispute what the tests concluded.
