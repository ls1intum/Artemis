How your work is marked depends on the exercise. A quiz and a programming exercise are marked by Artemis itself,
against the correct answers or the test cases. A text, modeling or file upload exercise is read and marked by a tutor
or an instructor. A programming exercise can be both: the tests give a starting point and a reviewer corrects it.

## Receiving Your Result

Nothing appears while marking is in progress. Once the assessment due date has passed, all the results are published
together, and the exercise header carries yours: the points you scored, your percentage, and the date up to which you
could dispute it.

<Image src={resultActions} alt="Exercise header with the points, the status and the complaint deadline" size={ImageSize.large} caption="Your result on the exercise page" />

Opening the status shows the feedback itself — the comments on your answer, the criteria that were applied, and where
points were added or taken off. For a programming exercise with immediate feedback, results appear during the working
period as well; whether that is switched on is your instructor's choice.

## Double-Blind Assessment

Marking is double-blind. The reviewer does not see your name while marking your work, and you do not see who marked
it. Neither of you can be influenced by the other's identity.

## Complaints

If you believe the assessment is wrong, **Complain** disputes it — where your instructor has enabled complaints. The
form asks you to say in detail why the assessment is wrong, in up to 2000 characters, and **Submit a complaint** sends
it. A **second** reviewer, not the one who marked your work, then looks at it.

<Image src={complaintForm} alt="Complaint form with its text box and the submit action" size={ImageSize.large} caption="Filing a complaint" />

Two limits apply.

- **A deadline.** The header names it as **Complaint due** — by default seven days after your result was published.
  After that the option is gone.
- **A number.** You may have only so many complaints that are still open or were rejected at once, three by default.
  An accepted complaint stops counting, so being right costs you nothing; being wrong repeatedly uses your allowance
  up.

A reviewer either accepts the complaint, which reopens the assessment and may change your marks, or rejects it and
explains why the original marks stand.

<Callout variant={CalloutVariant.warning}>

  Accepting a complaint reopens the whole assessment, not only the part you disputed, so a mark can go down as well as
  up.

</Callout>

A quiz result can never be complained about, and neither can Artemis's preliminary AI feedback. An automatically
marked exercise can be disputed only where your instructor has allowed it; if the option is off, no complaint deadline
appears in the header at all. In an exam, complaints are only possible during the student review period.

## More Feedback Requests

If you do not think the marks are wrong but want them explained, **Request more feedback** asks for that instead. The
form says what it does plainly: it does not affect your number of allowed complaints, but your score stays as it is,
and the **same** reviewer who marked your work answers.

<Image src={moreFeedbackForm} alt="More feedback request form, stating that the score stays unchanged" size={ImageSize.large} caption="Asking for more feedback" />

<Callout variant={CalloutVariant.danger}>

  Complaints and more feedback requests are mutually exclusive: Artemis keeps one or the other per result, never both.
  If you request more feedback you cannot complain about that result afterwards, even if it turns out the marking was
  wrong. When you are unsure whether the marks or only their explanation is the problem, complain.

</Callout>

## Rating Feedback Quality

Once you have your feedback, Artemis asks how useful it was, and the answer is five stars and nothing else — no
comment to write.

<Image src={feedbackRating} alt="Feedback rating asking how useful the feedback was, with five stars" size={ImageSize.large} caption="Rating the feedback you received" />

Rating changes nothing about your marks, and the reviewer cannot read what you gave them: only you and an
administrator can see an individual rating. What instructors see is the rating against the *reviewer's* name, never
yours, and each reviewer's average on the course leaderboard — which is how feedback that is not helping anyone gets
noticed. Rate on whether the feedback told you something you can use, not on the score it came with.
