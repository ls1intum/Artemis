Iris is a tutor built into Artemis that you can ask questions of while you work. It already has the exercise and
your latest submission in front of it, so you can ask about *this* task without explaining it first.

It is built to help you get there rather than to get there for you. Artemis does not allow it to give complete
solutions or full code blocks, whatever your instructor has configured — so asking for the answer will not produce
one, and asking why your answer is wrong usually will.

## Choosing Whether to Use AI

The first time you open anything AI-powered in Artemis, you are asked to choose. This is your decision, not your
instructor's, and it applies to your account.

<Image src={irisAiChoice} alt="Choose Your AI Experience, offering Cloud or No AI and a long-term memory toggle" size={ImageSize.large} caption="The choice you make once" />

**Cloud** uses current cloud models on Azure. Artemis states what that means: processing is GDPR-compliant, your data
is stored on university datacenters, and your personal details — your registration number, your email address — are
never sent to a model.

**No AI** leaves the rest of Artemis working exactly as it does now. You lose Iris, the automated AI feedback on
modeling and text exercises, and AI-powered guidance; you lose nothing else.

**Enable long-term memory** is separate and can be turned off on its own. With it on, Iris remembers your learning
preferences and past conversations so its help gets more personal over time. With it off, each conversation starts
from nothing.

<Callout variant={CalloutVariant.info}>

  Choosing **No AI** is a legitimate choice with no academic penalty attached: nothing that is marked depends on
  having used Iris.

</Callout>

The chat panel puts the same decision in front of you in words: accepting confirms that you are at least 18 and that
you agree to your interactions and the context around them — including your code — being processed, with nothing
stored in the cloud. Choosing an option settles both; **Decline** leaves the chat closed and changes nothing else
about Artemis.

## Asking a Question

On an exercise, the **Iris** tab sits in the panel beside the problem statement. Opening it gives you an empty chat
that already knows where you are.

<Image src={irisChatEmpty} alt="The Iris chat ready to use, with the current exercise set as its topic" size={ImageSize.medium} caption="The chat, with the exercise as its topic" />

The chip above the message box is the **topic** — the exercise Iris will answer about. The **+** button beside it
changes that: point Iris at a different exercise, at a lecture, or at the course as a whole when your question is not
about the task in front of you. The **×** on the chip removes the topic entirely, which is what you want for a
general question.

Type the question and send it. Iris answers in the panel, and you can keep going: it remembers the conversation, so
a follow-up like "why does that matter here?" works.

<Image src={irisConversation} alt="A question about the Observer pattern and Iris's reply" size={ImageSize.medium} caption="A question and its answer" />

Under each answer are three controls: one copies the text, and the thumbs up and down rate it. Those ratings feed an
aggregate that administrators can see, so they are how the people running Artemis find out that Iris is unhelpful.
They are worth using for that reason, and they do not reach your instructors as individual conversations.

<Callout variant={CalloutVariant.warning}>

  The panel says it plainly: Iris can make mistakes. It is confident whether or not it is right, so check anything
  that matters against the lecture, the problem statement or a tutor. Treat it as a well-read fellow student rather
  than as the marking scheme.

</Callout>

## Answers That Cite the Lecture

When Iris answers from lecture material, it places source chips beside the claims they support. Iris gives each chip
a short label and summary; its icon identifies a slide deck, a video transcript, or one of the course's FAQs.

Hovering a chip shows its summary. Lecture sources also show their **Unit:** and **Lecture:**. Where several sources
back the same sentence, their chips sit together as a group, and the arrows beside the summary step through them.

Clicking a lecture citation opens its unit, with the slides at the cited page or the video at the cited moment. That
lets you check an answer against the material: the chip shows where Iris found support, not whether its answer is right.

<Image src={irisCitation} alt="A source chip labelled Multithreading with its tooltip open, showing the summary above the unit Concurrency and the lecture Parallel Computing" size={ImageSize.medium} caption="A source chip with its tooltip open" />

### When a Citation Cannot Open the Exact Place

New citations to a lecture page or video moment carry an internal material version. When that version is available,
Artemis checks that the slides or video still match what Iris used before opening the exact position. A citation whose
version could not be verified opens the unit without jumping to that position. If a checked position cannot be opened,
Artemis tells you why:

- *The cited material has changed since this answer was written* — the slides or the video were replaced, so the page
  or the moment an older answer names may no longer hold the same content.
- *The cited material no longer exists* — the file or the video has been removed from the unit.
- *The cited material cannot be checked right now* — for example, while a video's transcript is being regenerated.

In all three cases the lecture still opens and only the exact position is left out. Once updated material has finished
processing, you can ask again for an answer based on it. If the whole unit has been deleted rather than its material
replaced, Artemis says that the linked content no longer exists and opens the lecture alone.

## Asking About What You Are Viewing

On a lecture page, Iris can use the slide pages visible in your open units as context for your next question. Once you
have played a video, it can also use the current video moment. In the combined view / fullscreen, Iris uses the one
unit open there. This viewing context is sent with your question, so you can ask "what is this right here?" without
naming the page. It reflects what was visible when you sent the message.

## When Iris Shows You the Place

Instead of describing where something is, Iris can take you there in the lecture unit's
[combined view / fullscreen](/student/learning-content/lectures#combined-view--fullscreen). It can move the slides to
a page or the video to a moment, then answer with that material in front of you. With synchronization on, the other
viewer may follow. The topic selector is hidden in this view; your next message uses the lecture that contains the
open unit.

When Artemis saves an acknowledged move, a chip appears in the conversation, reading *Navigated to page 5 and
timestamp 0:56 in lecture unit Concurrency*. The chip stays, so the place is one click away later on, and clicking it
reopens the view if you have closed it. If a saved point-out names both a page and a video moment that cannot stay
synchronized, **Synchronize slides and video** switches off so the next movement does not undo the point-out, and
Artemis names the page and the moment it is keeping.

<Image src={irisPointOut} alt="A question in the Iris chat, followed by a chip reading Navigated to page 5 and timestamp 0:56 in lecture unit Concurrency" size={ImageSize.medium} caption="The chip Artemis leaves after Iris moved the material" />

Iris can move the material only while that combined view / fullscreen is open. If you click a saved point-out after
the material has changed, the unit still opens, but the old position is withheld just as it is for a citation.

## Where Else Iris Appears

The exercise chat is offered on **programming** and **text** exercises. On other types — modeling, quiz, file
upload — there is no Iris tab, and in an **exam** there is none either, whatever the course settings say.

Beyond an exercise, Iris can answer about a **lecture** you have open, and about the **course** as a whole — which is
the one to use for "where do I find" and "what is due when" questions. Your instructor switches Iris on or off for the
whole course rather than place by place, so the difference between one exercise and another is its type, not a
setting.

Your conversations are yours: they are part of the
[data export](/student/tools-reference/exports#export-user-data-gdpr-data-export) you can request of everything
Artemis holds about you.

## Getting Better Answers

Iris is as good as the question. Three things help.

**Say what you tried.** "I expected the list to be sorted after the first pass but it is unchanged" gets you further
than "my code does not work", because the first tells Iris where to look.

**Set the right topic.** A question about last week's lecture asked with this week's exercise as the topic gets an
answer about the wrong thing. Change the chip first.

**Ask about the reasoning, not the answer.** Iris cannot give you the solution, so a request for one wastes a turn.
"Why is a list of observers better than calling each display directly?" is a question it can answer completely.
